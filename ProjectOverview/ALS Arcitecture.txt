AI-Driven Multi-Modal Brain MRI Fusion for ALS Classification


The architecture processes three co-registered MRI sequences: T1, T2, and FLAIR. From which, it produces a single probability representing the likelihood of ALS. Before entering the model, each sequence undergoes skull stripping and bias-field correction. T2 and FLAIR are rigidly registered to T1, while T1 is aligned to the MNI152 standard space. These transformations then place all three modalities within a shared anatomical coordinate system. After intensity normalization, the modalities are combined as a three-channel input.
A pretrained 3D convolutional encoder (CNN) is then used to extract spatial features from the MRI volumes. Each modality is processed using the same encoder weights, resulting in three modality-specific feature maps. These maps are concatenated and passed through a 1x1x1 convolution, which learns how to combine information across modalities into a unified feature representation, namely F. Note the encoder remains frozen on a cohort of this size, one trained from scratch tends to memorize the training subjects, therefore we keep the pretrained prior and train only the fusion and the layers after it.
Two views are then taken of F. The first is F itself, this is considered the spatial domain: where the learned features occur in the brain. The second is its frequency space, log(1 + |Fourier Transform(F)|). The Fourier Transform's magnitude spectrum represents how these patterns are distributed across different spatial frequencies. This enables the model to consider both anatomical structure and broader spatial characteristics of the learned features.
Both representations are then pooled into small grids, with each grid cell converted into a token. We take these resulting tokens from the two views, combine it into a single sequence, while an additional learned embedding identifies its respective source.
This sequence is processed by multiple Mamba layers, which model relationships between tokens. The model propagates the information forward rather than applying a fixed recurrence. The resulting token representations are averaged and passed through a linear classification layer to produce the final ALS probability. The classification threshold is determined using the validation set. Ablation experiments can remove the frequency-derived tokens and at the same time leave the remainder of the architecture unchanged. This provides a direct assessment of the contribution of frequency-domain information.
